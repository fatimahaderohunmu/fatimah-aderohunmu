Original Writing

Beyond the Ramp: Why Accessibility Must Be Part of Everyday Planning

Feature / Explainer · by Fatimah Adesola Aderohunmu · Original portfolio sample

Accessibility is often discussed as though it begins and ends with a ramp at the entrance of a building. While ramps, accessible toilets, lifts and parking spaces are important, inclusion is much broader than physical infrastructure.

For people with disabilities, ordinary activities can become difficult when accessibility is treated as an afterthought. Visiting a bank, attending an event, accessing public information or simply moving around a city may require additional planning because the environment was not designed with different needs in mind.

The digital world has added another layer to the accessibility conversation. Websites, mobile applications and social media platforms have become important channels for information and services. When digital content is not accessible, people with disabilities can be excluded even when a physical building is accessible.

Simple choices can make digital communication more inclusive. Clear language, descriptive alternative text for images, readable layouts, captions on videos and properly structured content can help more people access information. Accessibility should therefore be considered during content planning rather than added only after a problem is identified.

The same principle applies to public events. Organisers can consider accessible entrances, seating arrangements, sign-language interpretation, captioning, accessible toilets and clear information about the venue before inviting people to attend. These measures are not only about compliance; they can determine whether someone is able to participate independently.

There is also an important communication challenge. People with disabilities are often spoken about rather than included in conversations about the solutions that affect them. Involving people with lived experience in planning, testing and evaluating services can reveal barriers that may otherwise be overlooked.

Accessibility is ultimately about participation. A ramp can help someone enter a building, but meaningful inclusion asks a bigger question: once they are inside, can they participate fully? The answer should shape how organisations design spaces, services, technology and communication.

Building more accessible communities will require investment, awareness and better planning. It will also require a shift from treating accessibility as a special feature to recognising it as part of good design and effective communication.
